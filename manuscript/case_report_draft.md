## Abstract

Extraskeletal osteosarcoma (ESOS) is a rare, high-grade sarcoma that produces osteoid in soft tissue without skeletal attachment. It is classified among soft-tissue sarcomas yet is histologically indistinguishable from conventional osteosarcoma of bone, and whether it should be treated with bone-osteosarcoma or soft-tissue-sarcoma chemotherapy regimens remains unresolved; no randomized trial exists and molecular characterization at case level is sparse. We report integrated tumor–normal whole-exome and tumor whole-transcriptome analysis of a retroperitoneal ESOS in a woman in her mid-60s, re-analyzing a 2020 clinical dataset with current tools and cohort comparisons. Allele-specific copy-number analysis with two independent callers showed a whole-genome-doubled, highly aneuploid tumor (purity 0.57–0.67, ploidy 2.8–3.4; the requisition tumor-cellularity estimate was 90%) with homozygous deletion of TP53 and disruption of RB1 by copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity of exons 1–17 and a homozygous deletion from intron 17 whose inverted junction was resolved at base-pair resolution, accompanied by a clonal RB1 p.Thr502Ile variant of uncertain significance. The genome carried 17p11.2 and 8q gain and a high-copy (16–18 copies), apparently non-recurrent 2p11.2 amplicon encompassing KDM3A that was transcriptionally active across its ~5-Mb span. Point mutations were sparse (1.8 non-synonymous mutations/Mb by our criteria) except for a clonal APOBEC-like localized cluster of four strand-coordinated substitutions in MAP4K4, adjacent to an RNA-evidenced rearrangement, one of which the clinical report had listed as a variant of uncertain significance. No convincing oncogenic or sarcoma-defining fusion was detected. Against 88 TARGET-OS bone osteosarcomas and 259 TCGA soft-tissue sarcomas quantified with the same pipeline, the tumor's transcriptome was consistently classified as osteosarcoma (median Spearman ρ 0.64 vs ≤0.42 for any soft-tissue subtype; 25 of 25 nearest neighbours; reference-only classifiers with 99% cross-validated accuracy; 100% of 500 gene-resampling draws), with markedly reduced expression across the HOXA cluster relative to both bone osteosarcoma and retroperitoneal soft-tissue sarcoma. Deconvolution estimated a macrophage-rich microenvironment with median CD8 T-cell content; no HLA class I allele loss was detected. Research re-analysis found no pathogenic or likely pathogenic coding variant in cancer-predisposition genes. This ESOS showed striking genomic and transcriptomic convergence with conventional osteosarcoma while revealing case-specific structural and transcriptional features not captured by the original clinical analysis.

**Keywords:** extraskeletal osteosarcoma; osteosarcoma; TP53; RB1; whole-genome doubling; KDM3A; kataegis; RNA-seq; TARGET

## Introduction

Extraskeletal osteosarcoma accounts for roughly 1% of soft-tissue sarcomas and a few percent of all osteosarcomas [WHO 2020]. It arises most often in the thigh and retroperitoneum, in patients decades older than those with conventional osteosarcoma of bone, and carries a worse prognosis: in the largest series, 266 patients from the European Musculoskeletal Oncology Society, 5-year overall survival was ~47% [Longhi 2017]. Because it is defined by osteoid production in a soft-tissue location, its diagnosis is histological; but its biological identity — an osteosarcoma that happens to arise outside bone, or a soft-tissue sarcoma with osteoblastic differentiation — bears on management, since bone osteosarcoma is treated with methotrexate/doxorubicin/cisplatin-based regimens and soft-tissue sarcomas with doxorubicin/ifosfamide. Retrospective studies support a benefit of systemic chemotherapy in ESOS [Longhi 2017; Paludo 2018], and a 2025 propensity-matched registry analysis of 226 Japanese cases found chemotherapy associated with improved survival with no significant difference between osteosarcoma-type and soft-tissue-sarcoma-type regimens [Kondo 2025]; the optimal regimen class remains unresolved, and the disease is too rare for a randomized comparison.

Conventional osteosarcoma has a characteristic genome: near-universal inactivation of TP53 and frequent loss of RB1, extensive copy-number instability with chromothripsis and whole-genome doubling, recurrent gains at 6p12–21, 8q24, 17p11.2 and 19q12, localized hypermutation, and a striking absence of recurrent driver point mutations or fusions [Chen 2014; Perry 2014; Behjati 2017; Kovac 2015]. ESOS genomics is less developed but not blank: copy-number profiling and targeted sequencing of 32 cases found frequent CDKN2A, TP53 and RB1 losses, with concurrent biallelic TP53/RB1 loss in a subset associated with worse outcome [Jour 2016], and immunohistochemical/FISH analysis of 19 cases identified MDM2-amplified and H3K27me3-deficient subsets, indicating molecular heterogeneity [Makise 2018]; a recent case carried an ETV6::NTRK3 fusion [Skok 2025]. To our knowledge, however, no ESOS case has been reported with integrated tumor–normal exome, deep transcriptome, allele-specific copy-number reconstruction and expression-based classification against large osteosarcoma and soft-tissue-sarcoma cohorts.

Here we re-analyzed a clinical tumor–normal exome and transcriptome dataset generated in 2020 for a patient with retroperitoneal ESOS, using current annotation resources, allele-specific copy-number modelling, and like-for-like comparison with public osteosarcoma and soft-tissue-sarcoma cohorts. We asked what the clinical report had established, what it had not attempted, and whether the tumor's molecular identity is that of bone osteosarcoma.

## Results

### Clinical presentation and course

A woman in her mid-60s with no personal history of cancer presented in spring 2020 with right-sided abdominal pain and a palpable abdominal mass. Contrast-enhanced CT (day 0) showed a 12.6 × 10.3 × 7.6 cm retroperitoneal mass with heterogeneous enhancement, areas of necrosis and dense radiating central calcification, deviating the pancreatic head and uncinate process with suspected contiguous extension, obliterating the infrahepatic inferior vena cava (IVC), encasing the aorta near-circumferentially, and encasing the proximal right ureter with moderate right hydronephrosis; pelvic varicosities reflected IVC obstruction. There was no lymphadenopathy. The radiological differential was retroperitoneal sarcoma versus a pancreatic-head primary. A non-contrast chest CT (day 11) showed no pulmonary nodules, and an abdominal MRI (day 17) showed a 13 × 12 cm lobulated, predominantly solid mass encasing the aorta and obliterating the infrahepatic IVC, extending into the porta hepatis and displacing the pancreas anteriorly, with multiple simple hepatic cysts and no hepatic metastasis. An initial biopsy of the mass through the pancreatic head (day 7), reviewed at a tertiary sarcoma center, and a CT-guided core biopsy of the mass at that center (day 24) both showed **high-grade extraskeletal osteosarcoma**. Immunohistochemistry on the core biopsy was positive for SATB2, multifocally positive for smooth-muscle actin and positive in scattered cells for desmin, and negative for pan-keratin, AE1/AE3, MDM2 and CDK4, excluding dedifferentiated liposarcoma with osteosarcomatous differentiation and carcinoma. No skeletal origin was evident on imaging. Restaging CT at the sarcoma center (day 21) measured the mass at 14.9 × 12.2 × 10.9 cm with extension into the intrahepatic IVC and pancreatic head, new posterior extension into the right psoas, and worsening right hydronephrosis; bone windows showed only small, stable lucent lesions in the pelvis and proximal femur that were considered indeterminate and remained unchanged on all subsequent studies. Disease was therefore locally advanced without evidence of distant metastasis at diagnosis.

The tumor was judged unresectable because of its involvement of the IVC, aorta, pancreatic head, duodenum, right kidney/ureter and psoas, and primary radiation therapy was given: 25 fractions over approximately six weeks (days 31–73), interrupted by a ten-day admission during the eighth fraction for nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and pain. During that admission the mass measured 17 × 14 cm, bland thrombus was found in the distal IVC and right common iliac vein below the tumor-obliterated segment, and a small spontaneous hemoperitoneum developed; anticoagulation was started after radiation. Systemic chemotherapy was [not recommended / deferred / declined — reason if documented]. Symptom management included opioid analgesia and antiemetics through a palliative-care clinic. Post-treatment CT five weeks after the last fraction (day 112) showed the mass slightly decreased to 14.2 × 11.9 cm with reduced attenuation of its non-calcified components consistent with treatment effect, persistent invasion of adjacent organs and vessels, and slightly improved right hydronephrosis. CT at day 143 showed the mass further decreased to 13.1 × 8.2 cm, unchanged hydronephrosis, and interval enlargement of two indeterminate pulmonary nodules (to 8 mm and 4 mm) (Figure 6). [Subsequent course and outcome: records after December 2020 not yet reviewed.]

Clinical tumor–normal whole-exome and tumor whole-transcriptome sequencing (Sema4 Signal WES/WTS) was performed on the pre-treatment core biopsy block (day 24) and peripheral blood, with the report issued during radiation (day 60); it identified homozygous deletions of TP53 and RB1, a tumor mutational burden of 3.77 mutations/Mb, microsatellite stability, one variant of uncertain significance (MAP4K4 p.E589Q), no fusions, and no hereditary findings. The family later obtained the underlying data for research re-analysis [consent statement].

### Sequencing data

Tumor and normal exomes reached mean target coverages of 276× and 122×; tumor RNA (stranded total-RNA library; 51% of aligned bases intronic) yielded 327 million reads. Tumor cellularity per requisition was 90%.

### Germline

Research re-annotation of the normal-sample calls against ClinVar (2026-09-13), gnomAD v2.1.1 and Ensembl VEP 116 detected no pathogenic or likely pathogenic coding or splice variant in a prespecified panel of 51 cancer-predisposition genes (the ACMG secondary-findings cancer genes [v3.2; to be checked against v3.3] plus RECQL4, BLM, WRN, CDKN2A, POT1 and other sarcoma-associated genes), no rare protein-altering variant of uncertain significance in these genes, and no exon-level read-depth evidence of a heterozygous germline deletion of TP53 or RB1. Four ClinVar-pathogenic calls outside the panel were sequencing artifacts (allele fraction 0.17–0.20 at high depth in paralog-rich loci, or ≤5 reads). [One incidental heterozygous carrier-type variant outside the predisposition panel was identified and communicated for genetic counselling; details are withheld pending consent.] Exome data do not exclude deep-intronic, regulatory, mosaic or structural germline variants. The tumor's TP53 and RB1 losses were absent from the normal sample and are somatic.

### Purity, ploidy and allele-specific copy number

Allele-specific copy-number analysis with FACETS on 1.75 million common-SNP positions gave a tumor purity of 0.57 and ploidy of 3.4, stable across segmentation stringencies (Figure 1A; Supplementary Table S1). An independent analysis of the same allele counts with ASCAT gave purity 0.67 and ploidy 2.75, with a single optimum in the purity–ploidy likelihood surface and no near-diploid solution (Supplementary Figure S1); the two callers agreed on integer copy states at 9 of 11 assessed loci, differing only at the 46-kb TP53 deletion (below ASCAT's segmentation resolution) and in the amplitude of the 2p11.2 amplicon (16 vs 18 copies). Both classified the tumor as whole-genome doubled (>50% of the autosomal genome with major-allele copy number ≥2 [Bielski 2018]) and both estimated extensive loss of heterozygosity (27–32% of the genome). The requisition tumor-cellularity estimate was 90%; the clinical report expressed copy numbers relative to a diploid reference (segments called "normal" carry ~2.0 copies in the delivered segment file) and noted that they were approximate given aneuploidy. After allele-specific re-fitting, its "×1.5 losses" and "×2.5–3 gains" correspond to two-copy and ploidy-level states respectively. Focal amplification of 2p11.2 (hg19 chr2:83.08–88.13 Mb), which the clinical report had detected at approximately 8 copies, reached 16–18 copies and contained KDM3A (Figure 1B); 17p11.2 (AURKB, COPS3) was at 6–7 copies with LOH, and 8q (MYC) at 6 copies. RUNX2, VEGFA, CCND3 and CCNE1 were at ploidy level rather than gained. Single-copy loss with LOH affected PTEN, TSC2 and CDKN1C; copy-neutral LOH affected ATRX, STK11 and CHD5. An exome-derived genomic-scar analysis (LOH 19–20, TAI 31–33, LST 25–28; sum 75–81, using the definitions of Telli et al. [2016]) indicated a high scar burden; because this metric is not validated for osteosarcoma, is sensitive to segmentation and purity, and is inflated by whole-genome doubling, we regard it as provisional (Discussion).

### TP53 and RB1

TP53 was homozygously deleted across all 11 exons within a 46-kb focal deletion inside a 17p LOH block; the tumor:normal log-ratio (−2.1) matched the expectation for zero tumor copies at 57% purity (Figure 2A). RB1 was disrupted structurally (Figure 2B): exons 1–17 were retained at two copies with loss of heterozygosity, and exons 18 onward were homozygously deleted. Split and discordant reads resolved the deletion junction to chr13:48,986,411 in intron 17, joined in inverted orientation to chr13:49,237,832, ~180 kb beyond the 3' end of RB1 (36 split reads, 94 discordant pairs), indicating a fold-back-type rearrangement rather than a simple deletion. Because the 5' segment is present at two copies with LOH while the 3' segment is at zero copies, both retained copies of 13q carry the same junction, which most parsimoniously places the deletion before the duplication of that chromosome arm; the timing of the TP53 deletion, likewise present on both copies of a 17p LOH block, is inferred the same way and is less certain. In addition, a clonal somatic missense variant, RB1 c.1505C>T p.Thr502Ile (allele fraction 0.50, 58/117 reads; 0/55 in the normal; gnomAD maximum population frequency 1.9×10⁻⁴; SIFT tolerated, PolyPhen possibly damaging; conflicting germline classifications of uncertain significance/likely benign in ClinVar), was present on the retained 5' region; its multiplicity (1.5–1.8 copies across the two purity estimates) is consistent with, but does not prove, presence on both retained copies. We regard it as a variant of uncertain functional significance and do not rely on it for RB1 inactivation, which the homozygous deletion establishes. The clinical report did not list this variant. RNA supported the structural lesion: splice-junction reads across RB1 exons 18–27 were present at ~52% of the level across exons 1–17, close to the ~43% expected if 3' RB1 transcripts derive only from the non-tumor cells, and reads splicing from RB1 exons into the intron-17 rearrangement region were observed (Supplementary Figure S2).

### Somatic point mutations and a kataegis cluster

The delivered somatic call set contained 1,177 PASS calls, of which 1,018 carried a pipeline flag indicating that Mutect2 had filtered them and the vendor's post-processing had retained them; these were almost all below 5% allele fraction and frequently occurred as adjacent clusters within single read families. Applying an explicit filter (PASS; ≥8 alternate reads; allele fraction ≥0.05 for SNVs and ≥0.10 for indels; ≤0.02 in the normal; Methods) left 87 somatic calls (82 SNVs, 5 indels; Supplementary Table S2). Under this definition — non-synonymous calls in the filtered set divided by ~35 Mb of capture — the tumor mutational burden was 1.8/Mb; the clinical report stated 3.77/Mb under its own, unpublished definition, and the two figures are not directly comparable. Fifty-four calls were clonal, and 28 had multiplicity ≥2 in gained regions, i.e. predated the gain of their locus. The substitution spectrum (C>T 42, C>G 14, C>A 9 of 82) was consistent with age- and APOBEC-related processes but too small for formal signature decomposition. No somatic mutation affected BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, RAD51C/D, ATM, ATRX or other homologous-recombination or telomere-maintenance genes.

The only clustered event was in MAP4K4: four missense SNVs within 1.3 kb (hg19 chr2:102,476,129 G>A, 102,476,141 G>A, 102,476,171 G>C and 102,477,440 G>C; p.Glu503Lys, p.Ala507Thr, p.Glu517Gln and p.Glu620Gln on ENST00000314363; the last is the clinically reported p.Glu589Gln on NM_145686.3 — Supplementary Table S3), all G>A or G>C on the plus strand (i.e., strand-coordinated C>T/C>G), three of four in a TpC context, each at allele fraction 0.47–0.48 with no reads in the normal, and each on approximately three of the locus's five copies (Figure 3A). Three had been filtered by Mutect2 as clustered events. The independent STAR alignment of the RNA revealed 173 chimeric reads joining MAP4K4 exons to a single acceptor position ~600 kb downstream (within SLC9A2, which is not expressed), consistent with transcription across a genomic rearrangement whose breakpoint lies downstream of the last spliced MAP4K4 exon, in a window that includes the mutation cluster (Figure 3B). Four mutations fall short of the intermutation-distance definitions of kataegis, but their strand coordination, TpC context, identical allele fractions and proximity to a rearrangement are the features of an APOBEC-like localized mutation cluster at a rearrangement junction [Nik-Zainal 2012], a pattern described in osteosarcoma genomes [Behjati 2017]; we interpret them as a single mutational event rather than four independent driver mutations. Whole-genome sequencing would show whether additional clustered mutations flank the breakpoint.

### Fusions

The clinical finding of no clinically significant fusion was upheld: no convincing oncogenic or sarcoma-defining fusion was detected. Of 176 FusionCatcher candidates, none involved a sarcoma-defining partner (EWSR1, FUS, SS18, NR4A3, CIC, BCOR, NTRK1–3, HEY1–NCOA2) beyond artifact-level support; 43 were template-switching chimeras of COL1A1, the tumor's most abundant transcript; and the single FusionInspector-validated call (SEC31A–JAK2) had four junction reads, no spanning pairs and non-canonical splice sites. An independent STAR chimeric-junction analysis on 870,000 distant split reads confirmed the absence of canonical fusions and specifically excluded an ALK rearrangement (ALK 0.4 TPM; scattered non-canonical junctions). Several chimeric transcripts instead marked DNA copy-number boundaries: USP39–CTNNA2 (detected by both callers) joined the interior of the 2p11.2 amplicon to its flank, KDM3A–CYTOR was an intra-amplicon junction, and PGAP1–DNAH7 coincided with 2q segment boundaries (Figure 1B). Exome data could not confirm these junctions at the DNA level because the partner sites lie outside capture.

### Transcriptome: an osteosarcoma expression program with reduced HOXA-cluster expression

To compare the tumor with public cohorts on equal footing, tumor RNA was re-aligned and quantified with the GDC recipe (STAR two-pass, GRCh38, GENCODE v36; 163.7 million read pairs, 86.8% uniquely mapped) and compared with 88 TARGET-OS primary bone osteosarcomas and 259 TCGA-SARC primary soft-tissue sarcomas (leiomyosarcoma 101, dedifferentiated liposarcoma 57, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma 49, myxofibrosarcoma 25, MPNST 9, synovial sarcoma 10, other 8) from the UCSC Xena GDC hub, using rank-based methods to limit residual pipeline and library-preparation effects. Over the 2,000 most variable genes, the tumor's median Spearman correlation was 0.64 with TARGET-OS versus 0.42 with the closest soft-tissue subtypes (myxofibrosarcoma, UPS) and 0.36 with leiomyosarcoma; all 25 most similar cohort samples were osteosarcomas; centroid correlation was 0.75 for osteosarcoma versus 0.52 for the next group; and in principal-component space the tumor projected inside the osteosarcoma cluster (Figure 4A). The result was insensitive to normalization (identical on raw log-TPM ranks and after quantile mapping), to gene selection (TARGET-OS was the top group in 100% of 500 random draws of 1,000 genes from the 5,000 most variable; median margin over the runner-up 0.15, 95% interval 0.12–0.18), and to classifier choice: nearest-centroid and k-nearest-neighbour classifiers trained on the reference cohorts alone separated osteosarcoma from soft-tissue sarcoma with 99.4% and 99.1% leave-one-out accuracy and, applied afterwards to the ESOS sample, both assigned it to osteosarcoma (15 of 15 neighbour votes; Supplementary Figure S3). The comparison cohorts differ in age (TARGET-OS median 14 years; TCGA-SARC median 61) and site (TARGET-OS 86% lower limb; TCGA-SARC 38% retroperitoneal); the patient's age and tumor site resemble TCGA-SARC, so these confounders act against, not for, the osteosarcoma assignment. The osteoblastic program (SP7, IBSP, SPP1, ALPL, COL1A1, SATB2) was at typical osteosarcoma levels and 2–6 standard deviations above soft-tissue sarcomas; RUNX2 was at the 100th percentile of both cohorts without being amplified.

Genome-wide, the most over-expressed genes relative to osteosarcoma included six neighbours of KDM3A within the 2p11.2 amplicon (TGOLN2, ELMOD3, GGCX, MAT2A, POLR1A, PTCD3), indicating coordinate transcription across the amplified segment; KDM3A itself was 4.3 SD above the osteosarcoma mean (Figure 4B). Conversely, expression was markedly reduced across the HOXA cluster: every HOXA gene was below the osteosarcoma mean, with HOXA10, HOXA11, HOXA9 and HOXA5 at 2.7–3.4 SD below and near zero in absolute terms, together with HOXC9–11, PITX1 and MSX1, whereas HOXB genes were unremarkable and HOXD3, HOXD10 and HOXD11 were above the osteosarcoma mean (Figure 4C; Supplementary Table S4). The HOXA reduction was not explained by anatomical site: HOXA9, HOXA10 and HOXA11 were also 1.4–2.1 SD below the 98 retroperitoneal TCGA-SARC tumors, and lower than every anatomical subgroup of either cohort (Supplementary Figure S4). Smooth-muscle genes (MYOCD, ACTA2) were elevated relative to osteosarcoma but typical of soft-tissue sarcoma; given the multifocal smooth-muscle-actin positivity of lesional cells on immunohistochemistry, this signal is likely partly tumor-intrinsic as well as stromal. TERT was not expressed; ATRX was expressed above osteosarcoma levels while DAXX was reduced. PDGFRA and FGFR1 were at the 98th–99th percentile of osteosarcoma; CD276 (B7-H3) was expressed (57 TPM) but below the osteosarcoma median.

### Immune microenvironment and HLA

Computational deconvolution with MCP-counter and quanTIseq, run jointly on the tumor and both cohorts, estimated a myeloid-dominated microenvironment: the monocytic-lineage score exceeded that of every TARGET-OS sample, neutrophil and NK-cell scores were at the 91st–94th percentiles, and quanTIseq estimated a macrophage fraction of approximately 17% (M1 6%, M2 11%; osteosarcoma medians 1% and 7%). Estimated CD8 T-cell content was at the osteosarcoma median, CD4-type T cells were present, and CD274 (PD-L1) expression was low (Figure 5). These are transcriptome-derived estimates, not cell counts, and await immunohistochemical confirmation. HLA class I typing from normal DNA, tumor DNA and tumor RNA gave identical heterozygous genotypes at HLA-A, -B and -C; all six alleles had RNA support; and the HLA locus retained both parental alleles (3 total / 1 minor copy). No HLA class I allele loss was therefore detected, although TAP1 expression was below the osteosarcoma median and other components of antigen processing were not assessed functionally.

## Discussion

This retroperitoneal extraskeletal osteosarcoma showed striking molecular convergence with conventional osteosarcoma. Its genome carries the canonical osteosarcoma configuration — TP53 and RB1 inactivation by structural events, whole-genome doubling, extensive LOH, 17p11.2 and 8q gain, a localized APOBEC-like mutation cluster at a rearrangement, and no fusion or recurrent driver point mutation [Chen 2014; Behjati 2017] — and its transcriptome was classified as osteosarcoma rather than as any soft-tissue sarcoma subtype by every method we applied, on a common pipeline, with age and site confounders working against that assignment. The dedifferentiated liposarcoma alternative, the main mimic for a retroperitoneal bone-forming sarcoma and one of the molecular subsets described in ESOS [Makise 2018], is excluded three ways: MDM2 and CDK4 immunohistochemistry were negative on the diagnostic biopsy, neither gene is amplified, and neither is over-expressed; SATB2 positivity on the same biopsy corroborates the osteoblastic lineage inferred from expression. A single case cannot settle the classification of ESOS, which prior series suggest is molecularly heterogeneous [Jour 2016; Makise 2018; Skok 2025]; it can add one deeply characterized case in which the genome and transcriptome point the same way, and it is consistent with the frequent TP53/RB1 loss reported across ESOS cohorts [Jour 2016].

Three features are case-specific and of interest beyond this patient. First, a high-copy, apparently non-recurrent amplicon at 2p11.2 was transcribed across its ~5-Mb span. The clinical report had recognized the amplification and named KDM3A; the re-analysis refined its amplitude and structure and showed that its genes are coordinately over-expressed. KDM3A, an H3K9 demethylase, has functional support in osteosarcoma models, where it promotes proliferation, metastasis and glycolysis through SP1/PFKFB4 [Wang 2022] and mediates hypoxia-driven SOX9 activation [Li 2022], and in Ewing sarcoma [Parrish 2015]; it is the most plausible target of the amplicon, although several co-amplified genes are equally over-expressed and the amplicon's driver is not established. Chimeric transcripts at its boundary (USP39–CTNNA2, detected by two callers) and interior (KDM3A–CYTOR) corroborate a rearranged amplicon structure. Second, the MAP4K4 cluster illustrates a practical point for clinical re-analysis: a pipeline reported one of four adjacent clonal mutations as a variant of uncertain significance, whereas their strand coordination, TpC context, identical allele fractions and proximity to a rearrangement identify them as a single APOBEC-like mutational event with no implication of MAP4K4 as a driver. Third, expression across the HOXA cluster (and HOXC9–11) was markedly reduced relative to both bone osteosarcoma and retroperitoneal soft-tissue sarcoma, while HOXD10/11 were retained. Osteosarcomas carry HOX expression reflecting their skeletal site, and the retroperitoneum lies at lumbar level where HOX10 paralogs are normally expressed, so this pattern is not explained by anatomical position alone; whether it reflects the cell of origin, cluster-level epigenetic silencing, or the tumor's state is a hypothesis that requires anatomically matched osteosarcoma controls, age-matched comparisons and, ideally, methylation data at the HOXA locus.

The exome-derived genomic-scar analysis yielded a high LOH/TAI/LST burden without a homologous-recombination gene lesion. Kovac et al. reported that more than 80% of osteosarcomas exhibit a combination of substitution, LOH and large-scale instability signatures characteristic of BRCA1/2-deficient tumors [Kovac 2015], and osteosarcoma models with such features are sensitive to PARP inhibition in vitro [Engert 2017]. We do not classify this tumor as HRD-positive: the 42-point threshold derives from breast and ovarian cancer [Telli 2016], scar metrics migrated from arrays to sequencing are sensitive to tumor content, segmentation and preprocessing [Sztupinszki 2018; Nacer 2026], whole-genome doubling inflates the components, and no osteosarcoma-specific cutoff exists. A BRCA-like genomic pattern, formal HRD classification and clinically validated platinum/PARP sensitivity are distinct claims, and only the first is supported here; PARP inhibition is a research implication, not a treatment inference. The estimated microenvironment — macrophage-rich, CD8 at the osteosarcoma median, PD-L1 low, without HLA class I allele loss — is consistent with the generally limited activity of single-agent PD-1 blockade in osteosarcoma [Tawbi 2017; Le Cesne 2019], although the immune profile of an individual tumor is not a validated predictor of response; macrophage-directed and B7-H3-directed approaches, the latter under investigation in osteosarcoma CAR-T trials, are research directions rather than recommendations, and CD276 expression in this tumor was not above the osteosarcoma median.

Re-analysis also illustrates what a 2020 clinical report was designed to provide and what research reconstruction adds. The report correctly identified the TP53 and RB1 deletions and the 2p11.2/KDM3A amplification, recognized marked aneuploidy and stated that its copy numbers were approximate. Modern allele-specific re-analysis substantially revised absolute copy-number estimates and resolved copy-neutral LOH, whole-genome doubling, higher-order amplification and structural junctions not represented in the original report; it surfaced a clonal RB1 missense variant of uncertain significance; it required explicit re-filtering of the delivered somatic call set, most of which consisted of low-allele-fraction calls retained after Mutect2 filtering; and it used expression data that the clinical assay was not validated to interpret. None of this affected clinical management — the tumor was unresectable and was treated with radiation — but it is relevant to anyone re-using clinical sequencing files for research.

Limitations are those of a single case and of exome-plus-RNA data. Purity and ploidy were estimated by two callers that agree on whole-genome doubling and on gene-level copy states but differ along the purity–ploidy trade-off (0.57/3.4 vs 0.67/2.75); a third method or whole-genome sequencing would resolve this, and multiplicity-based timing inferences carry the corresponding uncertainty. The RNA library was total RNA whereas the comparison cohorts were poly-A, which we mitigated with rank-based methods and by excluding library-sensitive gene classes, and cohort age and site were not matched. Rearrangement junctions inferred from RNA could not be confirmed in exome DNA and await junction PCR or whole-genome sequencing. Germline copy-number screening lacked a panel of normals. Lineage immunohistochemistry (SATB2, MDM2, CDK4) was performed clinically and agrees with the genomic and transcriptomic findings; deconvolution estimates and the KDM3A amplicon await immunohistochemical validation (CD68, CD163, CD8, KDM3A) and, ideally, methylation-based classification on the archived block. The clinical course after the last available record (five months from diagnosis) has not yet been incorporated.

## Methods

**Samples and clinical sequencing.** Tumor tissue (FFPE block, retroperitoneal mass) and peripheral blood were sequenced in 2020 by Sema4 (Signal WES/WTS, CLIA-certified): Twist exome capture, NovaSeq 6000, GRCh37/hg19 alignment, Mutect2-based somatic calling, GATK4 somatic CNV, TruSeq Stranded Total RNA library, STAR alignment, RSEM quantification, FusionCatcher/STAR-Fusion/FusionInspector. Aligned reads, variant calls, segment files and quantifications were obtained by the family and re-analyzed.

**Germline.** Multi-allelic splitting and ClinVar (2026-09-13) annotation with bcftools 1.21; gnomAD v2.1.1 exome frequencies; Ensembl VEP 116 (GRCh37 cache, one transcript per variant, SIFT/PolyPhen, gnomAD MAX_AF). Variants were triaged by ClinVar classification and review status, population frequency, consequence, allele fraction and mapping-quality flags. A 51-gene predisposition panel comprised the ACMG SF v3.2 cancer genes plus sarcoma-associated genes. Germline copy number was screened by per-exon depth (mosdepth) in the normal relative to the sample median.

**Copy number.** snp-pileup on dbSNP common (b151) positions within exon targets; FACETS 0.6.2 [Shen 2016] at cval 150 and 300; ASCAT 3.2 [Van Loo 2010] on the same allele counts (LogR from depth ratio, BAF from alternate-allele fraction, germline heterozygous sites from the normal, ASPCF penalty 70, gamma 1); whole-genome doubling per Bielski et al. [2018]; genomic-scar components per Telli et al. [2016] re-implemented on exome segments; per-exon tumor:normal depth (mosdepth, normalised to genome-wide exon-target means); rearrangement junctions from split (SA-tag) and discordant reads (samtools).

**Somatic.** VEP 116 annotation of the delivered somatic VCF; filter as stated in Results (the pipeline's low-allele-fraction and reads-in-normal flags were replaced by explicit thresholds; its Mutect2-override flag was not used as an exclusion criterion because Mutect2's clustered-events filter removes genuine localized clusters); cancer-cell fraction and multiplicity from purity and local total/minor copy number [Dentro 2017]; localized clusters screened as ≥3 SNVs within 2 kb. TMB = non-synonymous filtered calls / 35 Mb.

**Expression.** Tumor RNA re-aligned from FASTQ with STAR 2.7.10b [Dobin 2013] two-pass against GRCh38 with GENCODE v36 using GDC parameters, gene counts converted to TPM with union-exon lengths; chimeric junctions recorded. Cohorts: TARGET-OS and TCGA-SARC STAR-TPM matrices and clinical tables from the UCSC Xena GDC hub [Goldman 2020], primary tumors only. Similarity by Spearman correlation over the 2,000 most variable genes, centroid correlation, and projection onto cohort principal components, computed on raw log2(TPM+1) and, as a sensitivity analysis, after quantile-mapping the tumor onto the pooled cohort distribution; reference-only nearest-centroid and k-nearest-neighbour (k = 15) classifiers with leave-one-out cross-validation within the cohorts before classifying the tumor; gene-selection bootstrap (500 draws of 1,000 genes from the 5,000 most variable); per-gene z-scores against each cohort and against anatomical subgroups (TARGET-OS by site; TCGA-SARC retroperitoneal vs limb); histone, small-RNA, mitochondrial and pseudoautosomal genes excluded from genome-wide lists.

**Fusions.** FusionCatcher [Nicorici 2014] output scored on unique junction reads, spanning pairs and artifact annotations; STAR chimeric junctions annotated to GENCODE genes as an independent check.

**Immune and HLA.** MCP-counter [Becht 2016] and quanTIseq [Finotello 2019] on the tumor and both cohorts jointly (GDC quantification); OptiType 1.5 [Szolek 2014] on MHC-region reads from normal DNA, tumor DNA and tumor RNA.

**Code and reproducibility.** All scripts, a step-by-step notebook with confidence tags and a corrections log are available at [repository URL]. Compute ran on a single AWS EC2 instance (~4 h total) and a laptop.
